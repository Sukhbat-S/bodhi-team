# Full discovery method

Use the smallest subset that can change the next action. Do not turn discovery into a fixed document template.

## Frame the opportunity

- Identify the narrowest real user group supported by evidence, not a demographic persona invented for completeness.
- Describe the struggling moment: trigger, context, desired progress, friction, and consequence.
- Remove the proposed feature from the problem statement. If the problem disappears with the feature name, the frame is still solution-first.
- Identify the current alternative, including manual work, delay, delegation, partial workarounds, and doing nothing.

## Build the evidence ledger

Separate:

- **Observed behavior:** what people actually did, attempted, abandoned, repeated, paid for, or switched from.
- **Commitment:** time, money, data, access, reputation, workflow change, or another meaningful cost they accepted.
- **Testimony:** what people say they want, remember, predict, or prefer.
- **Assumption:** a belief necessary for the opportunity to work but not yet supported.
- **Unknown:** missing evidence that could change the next action.

Retain source, date, population, and relevant limitation for consequential evidence. Do not convert anecdotes, compliments, sign-ups, or survey enthusiasm into demand without an observable commitment.

## Diagnose the opportunity

Evaluate only the dimensions that remain uncertain:

- **Problem:** frequency, severity, urgency, current cost, and whether the user recognizes the struggle.
- **Value:** whether solving it creates enough progress to motivate switching or commitment.
- **Usability:** whether the intended user can understand and complete the critical behavior.
- **Feasibility:** whether the smallest useful solution can be delivered under current technical and operational constraints.
- **Business viability:** whether acquisition, delivery, support, economics, incentives, and strategic fit can work together.

Rank assumptions by probability of being wrong multiplied by consequence if wrong. Choose the riskiest assumption, not merely the easiest one to test.

## Design the smallest valid experiment

State:

- The assumption being tested.
- The target participant and real context.
- The behavior or commitment that would count as evidence.
- The minimum exposure needed to interpret the result.
- A success threshold, disconfirmation threshold, and ambiguous zone set before observing results.
- The cheapest honest test that preserves the uncertainty being measured.
- The review date or event and the next action for each outcome.

Prefer existing behavior, transaction evidence, concierge delivery, prototype tasks, commitment tests, or reversible pilots over opinion surveys. Do not use a prototype when the uncertainty is whether the problem exists; do not build production software when a manual test can expose the same risk.

## Check second-order effects

Before recommending build or scale, examine who must change behavior, who bears new work, what existing workflow or trust is displaced, how the result could be gamed, and which downstream cost appears only after adoption.

Return one recommendation: investigate, test, build, reposition, or stop. Include the evidence that would reverse it and a clear kill or review trigger when further investment is material.
